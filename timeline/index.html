The dead kept in the cave

Neanderthals repeatedly carried their dead into one cave in northern Iraq and put them down in the floor. Shanidar 4 was found on his side with his arm under his head; Shanidar Z lay in a hollow gouged out to fit her, with a stone under her head. The cluster dates to roughly seventy to seventy-five thousand years ago.

The oldest evidence on this site of people treating the dead as somewhere you go, rather than something you leave behind. It sits about fifty-five thousand years before the dog, which settles an argument this site was getting wrong: one-sided attachment to a non-human does not begin with a domesticated animal. It is also the discipline case. The famous flowers were a mistake - the pollen turned out to come from burrowing rodents - and the burials survived the debunk anyway. That is what a real claim looks like after someone has been wrong about part of it. One limit worth stating: Sunghir, later and better studied, shows this was not a single uniform practice, so what Shanidar documents is the earliest instance of something that varied a great deal.

The carved animals

Upper Paleolithic carvers in Europe made small figures of animals and people from mammoth ivory from about forty thousand years ago. The Vogelherd set includes a cave lion, a bison, a horse, a bear, a mammoth, and a lion-man - a body with a human face on a lion's. At La Madeleine in France a bison carved from antler is caught mid-grooming, turning its head to lick its own side.

This is what the dog entry used to deny. Animals carrying human qualities were being made, kept and shown to other people tens of thousands of years before the dog: the grooming gesture is specifically human, and somebody carved it in antler on purpose. It also puts an anthropomorphised animal on the same taxonomy shelf as the dog roughly twenty-five thousand years ahead of it, which is the honest shape of the argument. The dog is not the first animal to be given human shape in the imagination. It is the first animal to be bred into living alongside us.

c. 9500 BCE · Myth & Legend

Close ×

Eleven thousand years of pillars

Round enclosures on a hillside in Anatolia holding T-shaped pillars carved to suggest human figures, built by people who had not yet domesticated a single animal - used for over a thousand years, then deliberately filled in and built over.

The oldest thing on this site with a claim to be about figures, and the interpretation is genuinely open, which is the point. What is not in dispute: hunter-gatherers cut limestone into anthropomorphic forms with stone and bone tools, set them upright in circles, carved them with foxes, scorpions, lions and vultures, returned to them for fourteen hundred years, and then filled the enclosures in on purpose. There is no hearth, no rubbish pit and no sign that anyone lived there - people travelled to the place. UNESCO's own nomination describes the pillars as believed to represent 'ancestors, perhaps even incipient deities', and other readings have them as totemic or protective figures. Nobody has settled it, and the carvings include a great many frightening animals: the dominant imagery is not a kind face but lions, spiders, snakes and scorpions. What this site can claim is narrow and still large: for at least a millennium, people made representations of beings, went out of their way to be near them, and eventually chose to close the place where they stood. Whether the beings were ancestors is the question this entry cannot answer, and saying so is more useful here than guessing.

c. 800 BCE · Myth & Legend

Close ×

Homer and the voice in the room

The Homeric poems were performed, not read: a bard recited a familiar story to a room that already knew the characters and the outcome.

The audience did not meet Homer; it met a voice performing a world it already knew. The bard's presence, the formulaic epithets and the repetition made the heroes available as figures to admire, mourn and argue about. The bond is with the performance and with the persona of the singer as much as with Achilles. It is the earliest recorded structure in which a crowd gives its attention to one voice and receives a story in return.

The recitation is also a schedule. A poem that takes hours to perform holds a room together for that long, and the audience's memory carries the characters between performances. That continuity is the pre-print version of the serial: the figure exists because a community keeps returning to the same voice.

c. 250 BCE–550 CE · Myth & Legend

Close ×

The wax masks in the hall

A Roman aristocrat had a wax mask of his own face taken while he was alive - usually in his thirties or forties, on taking office - and it was kept in a cupboard in the entrance hall beside a painted family tree and a label listing his offices. He never saw it worn. After his death, actors put it on and walked it in his funeral procession, in order of death, wearing the robes of the office he had held.

The best-documented one-sided attachment in this corpus, and thirteen thousand years later than the dog - which is the point. Everything here is checkable, because it was written down, and what it shows is how completely a bond can be built with someone who cannot answer. The mask never speaks: eye-holes and a gap between the lips were cut into it so that the actor could see and talk, and the inscription beside it did the conversing. Children grew up walking past it daily - Valerius Maximus has a man recalling that he called the dead his parents and drew his standing from their masks - and it had states: opened at festivals, hung with laurel on an election, closed during mourning. It was also an inheritance that changed address, going with a bride into her husband's house, and it was not uniformly flattering: Pliny's son was described as burdened by the masks he carried. No mask survives. All of it comes from authors.

c. 8 CE · Myth & Legend

Narcissus and Echo: the image that cannot answer

Ovid's Metamorphoses tells of a nymph cursed to repeat only the last words spoken to her, and a youth who falls in love with his own reflection in a pool.

Echo has a voice but no sentence of her own: Juno's curse leaves her able to return the end of another person's speech, so every reply is borrowed. Narcissus is answered, but only by a surface that mirrors his own approach; when he reaches for it, it breaks. The myth separates two mechanisms the rest of this timeline keeps finding together: a far end that can only repeat, and a far end that can only reflect.

The attachment is intense because the near side supplies both halves of the exchange and misreads the result as consent. Ovid's sharper point is that Narcissus is not simply fooled. He loves an image he cannot touch, and the image is accurate. The tragedy is not a failure to tell reality from representation; it is an attachment that survives the distinction.

The priest

A person who hears the specifics, performs the ritual, and knows the family history, on a repeating cycle.

The most complete parasocial arrangement available and it includes a uniform, a script and a confession. It is also the arrangement the most people are inside of, which is the strongest evidence that the form is universal rather than a symptom.

787 CE · Myth & Legend

Close ×

The icon answers nothing

In 787 the Second Council of Nicaea ended the first phase of the Byzantine iconoclastic dispute and defined what an icon was for: the honour paid to the image passes to the figure it shows. Icons were lawful because in Christ the invisible God had been seen. The panel itself never answered anyone.

The council drew a line only the second phase of iconoclasm would redraw. The Byzantine dispute over images ran from the reign of the emperor Leo III in the 730s; the Second Council of Nicaea, summoned in 787, closed the first phase by declaring the veneration of icons lawful and orthodox. Its formula rests on a distinction the church still repeats: relative veneration (proskynesis - the honour, the greeting) was owed to the image, while true worship (latreia) belonged to God alone. The classical backing was Basil of Caesarea's sentence that the honour paid to the image passes to the prototype - the figure the image shows.

What the icon is for makes this site's subject in paint. The devotee stands before a panel understood, in the argument of John of Damascus and later the Studite writers, to participate in the figure it shows, so that a prayer said 'through' the image is addressed to somebody who is not in the room. The venerated panel cannot answer, and nobody expects it to; the whole doctrine runs on the reply being withheld here and heard elsewhere. The icon is a face in a church that speaks to nobody back - the painted absence this corpus already records in the empty-wall entry, arriving as an official theology rather than a habit.

The counter-argument is the one the council had to defeat, and it is kept on the entry rather than resolved. The iconoclasts charged the practice as idolatry; the council's answer was that honour directed through an image is not the worship of wood and pigment, and the plain reading of the ban on graven images was set against the Incarnation - the unseeable God had been seen in Christ. The settlement did not hold: the icons were banned again in 815 and restored for good only in 843. One political parallel the same argument used is worth keeping - the imperial portrait honoured an absent emperor exactly as an icon honours an absent Christ, which is the civic version of the same structure.

Dante's Beatrice: the woman who becomes a guide

It is the condition of the poem's vision, and the reader is asked to accept the substitution as the price of admission.

c. 1327–1374 · Literature & Print

Petrarch's Laura and the poetry of absence

Petrarch wrote hundreds of poems to Laura, a woman he may have seen once, and made the unreachable beloved the centre of a literary career.

Petrarch's Laura is a name and a face that the poems repeatedly approach and never possess. The sequence is built on absence: the speaker addresses a woman who cannot reply, and the reader is placed in the same posture. The sonnet form becomes a technology for keeping the attachment alive after the occasion has gone. It is the template for centuries of love poetry in which the beloved is a figure the reader is invited to desire.

The poems do not pretend the relationship is mutual. They make the non-answer the subject, and the reader's pleasure is in the precision of the longing. That is a different mechanism from the novel's immersive identification: here the distance is not a problem to be solved but the instrument that produces the feeling.

c. 1387-1400 · Literature & Print

Close ×

Chaucer's 'I': a narrator who is not the author

The Canterbury Tales frame a company of pilgrims with a first-person narrator, 'Chaucer', who reports their words and his own embarrassment.

The General Prologue creates a voice that is warm, gossipy and slightly foolish, and it addresses the reader as a companion on the road. The historical Chaucer is not that voice. The poem's 'I' is a persona who misremembers, apologises and invites the reader to judge the pilgrims alongside him. That gap is the first durable English example of mediated intimacy: the reader is spoken to by a constructed person who seems to have no screen between himself and the page.

The contract is conversational rather than dramatic. The narrator says he will tell the tale as he heard it and asks indulgence for plain speech, which positions the reader as a listener who has been let in. The frame does not require belief in the narrator, only the pleasant illusion that someone is talking to you rather than at you.

1531 · Myth & Legend

Close ×

Am I not here, I who am your mother?

Four apparitions to a Chichimec farmer and one to his uncle, a single Nahuatl sentence that gave the devotion its title, and a cloth image the Catholic world has organised itself around ever since.

The entire relationship is address, and the founding story is one reply. According to the Nican Mopohua, written in Nahuatl, the Virgin appeared four times to Juan Diego, a Chichimec peasant, and once to his uncle Juan Bernardino, beginning on the morning of 9 December 1531. She spoke to him in his own language - Nahuatl, the language of the former Aztec empire - and identified herself as the mother of the very true deity. The sign he was sent to gather was Castile roses, not native to Mexico, blooming out of season on the summit of Tepeyac Hill; he carried them in his tilma, the cloak that bears the image venerated ever since. And the sentence the devotion is built on is her answer to his worry: '¿No estoy yo aquí que soy tu madre?' - 'Am I not here, I who am your mother?' - which the record calls the most famous phrase of the apparitions, and which is inscribed above the main entrance to the Basilica of Our Lady of Guadalupe, on Tepeyac Hill in Mexico City, the most-visited Catholic shrine in the world and, the article says, the third most-visited sacred site. The shelf's test is address rather than watching, with the other end understood to hear, and that is the whole cult - built on one reply that nobody but a single man is recorded to have received. The evidence has been contested from the beginning. The 1556 inquiry records an attack on devotion to an image 'painted yesterday by the Indian Marcos'; Sahagún recorded his suspicion that it was 'a Satanic invention to cloak idolatry under the confusion of this name, Tonantzin' - 'They come from distant lands to this Tonantzin as in olden times'. Juan Diego himself is doubted: Zumárraga's own writings never mention him, the 1556 inquiry omits him, he appears in no document before the mid-17th century, and in 1996 the abbot of the Basilica was forced to resign after saying Juan Diego was 'a symbol, not a reality'. None of it stopped anything. In 1754 the Sacred Congregation of Rites confirmed the apparitions and the December 12 feast; Leo XIII granted the decree of canonical coronation in 1887, executed in 1895; and in 2002 Juan Diego was canonised as the first indigenous American saint. The image's own history is a record of being handled, defended and examined. In 1887-88 the ten-point crown painted on the Virgin's head disappeared - the change was noticed on 23 February 1888, and eventually a painter confessed on his deathbed that a clergyman had instructed him to remove it. In 1921 a bomb left under the tilma by an anti-Catholic secularist damaged the altar and left the image unharmed; the survival is called miraculous by devotees. In 1981, examining the cloth under infrared light, Philip Serna Callahan and Jody Brant Smith found no trace of sizing or sketching underneath the paint, and no explanation for the original parts of the image or their preservation. That is the honest shape of the whole entry: the most-visited shrine in the Catholic world rests on a Nahuatl text and a piece of cloth, and the address has been going up to both, undiminished, for five hundred years.

Don Quixote reads himself into a knight

Cervantes's Alonso Quixano reads so many chivalric romances that he resolves to become a knight-errant, and the world has to negotiate with the fiction he is living in.

Quixote is not merely a fan of romances. He uses them as a script for conduct, renaming himself, arming himself and interpreting inns as castles. The parasocial structure runs in the opposite direction from most of this timeline: instead of attaching to a distant figure, he attaches to a genre and then demands that the genre's rules be honoured by everyone he meets.

Cervantes makes the reader complicit. We are given the fiction and the correction in the same sentence, so we occupy the position of the sane household and the position of the man who prefers the story. That double occupancy is the earliest sustained literary account of what it feels like to know a representation is not real and to keep acting as if it were.

1740 · Literature & Print

Close ×

Virtue rewarded

The novel that turned its readers into a correspondent. Pamela Andrews writes home about her master's pursuit, and the reader takes the seat of the parents - receiving every letter, sending none. Published anonymously in November 1740, it became the first English best-seller and the first novel whose readers wrote back demanding more of it.

The form is a correspondence the reader reads from the far end of. Richardson was a printer who took a commission in 1739 to compile a volume of model "familiar letters" for people too unskilled to write their own, and the germ of Pamela was a short run of letters from a servant daughter asking her father's advice about her master's advances. He kept the epistolary shape and changed its address: the letters are written not to a lover but to Pamela's parents, who are offstage, at a distance, and never answer inside the book. The reader is put in that seat - handed a life in letters addressed to somebody else, with no way to write back - which is this site's subject arriving in the first novel most of its readers ever meet.

The reception is a record of attachment, and it is unusually well documented. The book is, on the Cambridge edition's own description, "perhaps the most influential novel published in Britain in the eighteenth century" and "on its first publication in 1740 ... an immediate bestseller"; it ran to five editions in its first year and produced what the University of Michigan project calls a "'Pamela' rage," with Pamela motifs on teacups and fans. The world was said to divide into "Pamelists and Antipamelists." A village blacksmith, the oft-repeated anecdote runs, read it aloud on his anvil through the summer evenings to an attentive crowd and set the parish bells ringing at the happy ending. And the readers did not stay passive: the unauthorized continuations and parodies - Fielding's Shamela, Haywood's Anti-Pamela, Kelly's Pamela's Conduct in High Life - drew a sequel from Richardson himself, Pamela in her Exalted Condition, in 1742. The audience's want bent the text.

The counter-argument is that Pamela belongs to the history of the novel, not to this site: its importance is form, consent and class, and the people who wrote back wrote to Richardson, not to a girl who never existed. It is a fair objection and the entry's claim is narrower than it looks. What it files is a structure - a one-sided correspondence the reader reads from the parents' end - and a documented public that formed an attachment and then wrote to keep it going. The letters in the book are addressed to figures who cannot reply; the letters out of the book were addressed to a man who could, and did, and wrote two more volumes because of them. Both are this site's shape. Contestable, and recorded as such.

1774 · Literature & Print

Close ×

Werther and the costume of grief

Goethe's epistolary novel made a young man's unrequited love and suicide a European sensation, and readers copied both the feeling and the clothes.

Werther writes letters to a friend and to Lotte, and the reader is placed inside an intimacy that has only one correspondent. The novel's form is the mechanism: no omniscient narrator corrects him, so the reader lives at his level of certainty. When the book became a fashion, the clothing style described in it turned a private attachment into a public costume, and the reported copycat deaths gave the reception its later name, the Werther effect.

The important point is not that readers were foolish. It is that print can distribute a whole emotional grammar - what to feel, what to wear, how to die - and that the grammar arrives with the intimacy of a letter. The copycat reports are themselves disputed; Goethe's biographer Rüdiger Safranski called the Werther effect a persistent rumour, which is the right caution for a story the culture wanted to be true.

Lord Byron woke up famous after Childe Harold's Pilgrimage and became the first poet whose readers treated the author as the hero of his own work.

1830s-1850s · Literature & Print

Dickens, the serial, and the death of Little Nell

Dickens published his novels in monthly and weekly parts, making the next instalment a shared appointment; the death of Little Nell in The Old Curiosity Shop became a public bereavement.

Serial publication turned reading into a schedule. A huge audience met the same characters in the same week, and the gap between parts was a real interval in which the characters continued to exist without the reader. That interval is the engine: it produces anticipation, conjecture and a sense of ongoing relationship rather than a single transaction.

The death of Nell made the mechanism visible. Readers mourned a child who had never existed, and the story that American crowds met the incoming ship asking whether she had died is repeated so often that it has become part of the reception even where the evidence is thin. Dickens's later public readings, where audiences were reported to faint at the murder of Nancy, moved the same attachment into a room with a live body in it.

Lisztomania and the souvenir body

Franz Liszt's concert tours produced scenes of fainting, souvenir-collecting and crowd frenzy that the press named Lisztomania.

Liszt played in public and then let the public take pieces of him: gloves, handkerchiefs, locks of hair, broken piano strings. The performance was not only the music but the availability of the performer's body. Heinrich Heine coined the term, and the pattern it describes - a crowd treating an artist as a personal possession - is the direct ancestor of the pop idol.

The souvenir is the mechanism made physical. A glove or a string is not the person, but it is a piece of the person's presence that can be carried away. The audience's attachment is to a body it has seen at a distance, and the object is the substitute for a reply.

Jane Eyre speaks to one reader at a time

Charlotte Brontë's Jane Eyre addressed the reader directly in its opening pages and made a fictional woman's interior life a mass reading experience.

The novel begins with 'There was no possibility of taking a walk that day', and the reader is placed inside Jane's head before any plot arrives. 1891 · Music & Fan Culture

Close ×

The Divine Sarah

Sarah Bernhardt toured more like a head of state than a player: when she sailed for New York in 1891 the crossing was covered the way a royal visit is, and 'Divine Sarah' was by then the whole public's phrase for her. Mark Twain's note put it exactly - there were five kinds of actresses, and Sarah Bernhardt. An idol built at distance.

The most famous face the pre-cinema stage could make. Born in Paris in 1844, Bernhardt was by the 1870s the figure the international press called Divine Sarah - Oscar Wilde's 1879 poem of that name had her crowned with lilies at the feet of audiences who would never see her act in a room, and a French paper called her a national institution. Her motto was 'Quand Même' - 'in spite of everything' - which the torments of a career turned into a legend of refusal.

The 1891 tour was the idol arriving. In 1891 she left Le Havre on 15 October aboard the L'Amérique on her way to America, and the arrival was covered in the register of a state visit: one account of these tours records the homage shown her as the kind 'usually reserved for royalty', applause and press for a woman most of the auditorium had only met through prints, reviews and playbills. Distance is the mechanism - the audience knew the surface better than the woman, and a finished public surface is exactly what a parasocial idol is made of.

She stood at the seam between the printed and the cinematic star. Mark Twain's tribute, written for a tour programme, ranked her apart from every other class of actress, and the cinema that arrived within a generation inherited the structure: the same manufactured distance, the same schedule of manufactured encounters, the same audience that never arrived in time for the making. The counter-argument - that she was simply the greatest actress of her age and the devotion was earned on stage - is kept on the entry. The claim here is narrower: her fame was distributed by the print machinery this corpus files everywhere else under the idol, which makes her the theatre's first systematic case of it.

A doodle of you used as your address

By 1917 nine out of ten men at a costume ball came as him, a hold-up man in Cincinnati borrowed his costume as a disguise, and in 1916 somebody was paging him in more than eight hundred hotels at the same moment. Later, fans sent letters with a doodle of his face where the address should have been. The likeness had stopped being a picture and started being an instruction.

The silhouette was usable. That is the entry. The Tramp first appears in Kid Auto Races at Venice, filmed on 11 January 1914 and released on 7 February; by 1917 the costume was common enough to be a costume of record - "most of the girls came as Annette Kellerman, the swimming star, and nine out of ten men came as Charlie Chaplin." In February 1917 the same costume was used as a disguise by a hold-up man in Cincinnati. A likeness that exists only on a screen cannot be worn by a robber. This one could, and that is the difference between being famous and being an instruction.

The same period that produced the robbers produced the diagnosis. About 12 November 1916 the Boston Society for Psychical Research was investigating "certain phenomena connected with the simultaneous paging of Mr Charles Chaplin, motion picture comedian, in more than 800 large hotels of the United States" - one man being asked for in eight hundred hotels at once, from the Atlantic to the Pacific and the Canadian boundary to the Gulf - and the Kansas City Star asked the next day whether readers had had "the Chaplin-itis?" Robinson's account of the period has theatres "promoting the Chaplin vogue by sponsoring amateur Charlie look-alike contests"; Chaplin himself said he was "tempted to give lessons in the Chaplin walk."

The contest story is told two ways and both are here. The Chaplin archive's own press clippings from 1918 record Mary Pickford telling a dinner in London that he had entered a Chaplin walk contest at a fair in the United States and came in twentieth; the version everybody repeats has him coming in third, which Snopes rates Unproven and traces to a magazine post from December 2022. The famous version is the one that needed no source, and the documented one is second-hand from a friend. Neither number changes what the contests were for: an audience competing to be the nearest copy of a man who was not there to judge it.

The mail is where the likeness becomes infrastructure. In a 1932 feature on "Hollywood's Funny Fan Mail", Clara Bow was receiving about 35,000 letters a month; Maurice Chevalier's arrived with no address whatever, just his picture on the envelope; Clark Gable's were addressed to "Valentino the Second". An address that works without a street is a likeness doing a job, and by the 1950s a doodle of Chaplin's face was being used as the address on letters sent to him at the Savoy Hotel. This entry does not claim what became of that post; the point is only that it was thought to be enough to send.

Known to more people than any other woman in history

The first film performer whose value could be counted instead of guessed. She was on the cover of the New York Dramatic Mirror in 1911, an honour then kept for theatrical stars; by 1916 her fan following had become ten thousand dollars a week, half her film profits and a production company of her own. The audience was never asked for any of it, and every line of the contract exists because of it.

The fan worship is in the contract. St. John's line, as the American Experience page quotes it, is the scale the entry works from: Pickford was "known to more people and loved by more people than any other woman that has been in all history." Reach was the asset, and reach was countable. In 1911 she was the first movie star to adorn the cover of the New York Dramatic Mirror, an honour "previously bestowed only on theatrical stars" - the trade press had already decided that a moving-picture player could carry a cover, and the sentence that records it is careful to say what the honour used to be worth.

Then 1914 and Tess of the Storm Country. The record says the picture "caused a sensation", that it "created fan worship that had never before been witnessed", and that the success "gave Mary Pickford incredible bargaining power"; by 1916 she had "a $10,000 a week salary, 50% of her film profits, and her own production company." The chronology is kept rather than resolved: her foundation dates the renegotiation to May 1916, another account dates a contract at 24 June 1916 with a guarantee of $1.04 million. Both are here, because a date and a number that disagree are the ordinary state of a contract nobody was meant to read. United Artists followed in February 1919, with Fairbanks, Chaplin, Griffith and Hart.

The other side of it is why this is not a portrait. When the company she co-founded had its way, it would "pressure Pickford to limit herself artistically, playing roles she knew her fans would love." Nobody voted and the studio obeyed anyway. That is the shape of the whole period: an audience made sovereign and unaware of it, consulted through a contract clause rather than through a question. By 1927 the highs were over, and what the constraint had been buying was visible from the outside.

1921 · Broadcast & Film

Close ×

Cordially yours, Betty Crocker

In 1921 a flour company's advertising department needed one woman's name for the kitchen advice its all-female home-service staff was already writing. Betty Crocker was a signature and a persona, not a person: she answered letters, then took a radio voice in 1924, and gave millions a correspondent who signed herself "Cordially yours."

The reply existed before the name did. In late October 1921 Gold Medal Flour ran a jigsaw-puzzle advertisement in The Saturday Evening Post; Washburn-Crosby received 30,000 completed puzzles and, with them, several hundred letters of cooking questions. The advertising department asked the board to create a personality that the women answering the letters could all sign, as the company's own account puts it. "Crocker" honoured a retired company director, William G. Crocker; "Betty" was chosen because it sounded friendly; employees competed to submit the signature and Florence Lindberg's was adopted, and is still in use. The replies, written by a home-service department of home economists, went out signed "Cordially Yours, Betty Crocker" - a real hand typing under a name no woman owned.

Then the persona was given a voice, which is what makes it a bond rather than a byline. In 1924 the company helped a local radio station out of bankruptcy, renamed it WCCO, and put Betty Crocker on daytime radio's first cooking show, "the Betty Crocker Cooking School of the Air"; Blanche Ingersoll was its first voice, the program spread to thirteen regional stations, and from 1927 it ran on the fledgling NBC network for twenty-four years, with more than a million listeners enrolled. Each station had its own Betty Crocker reading the same scripts from Minneapolis, and letters - now from across the Midwest - kept arriving. General Mills' own description of the effect is this entry's best line: her voice on the air "was like the woman next door dropping in for a morning visit to share a new recipe."

This is the brand-as-character mechanism at its founding, decades before the corpus had a name for it. A corporation was given a face and a tone so that a customer relationship would have a subject, and the entry files it as an instance of that later mechanism with the practice dated to the year the signature was drawn. The tie is one-sided in the way that matters: the reader attached to a woman who did not exist, and the reply, however real the labour behind it, came from the persona rather than the person. The counter - that a home economist really did answer, so the bond was two-way - loses because the far end of the reader's attachment was Betty, and Betty could not answer as herself; the correspondence ran under a single invented name for exactly that purpose. Contestable, and recorded as such.

Most faithfully, Rin Tin Tin

A German Shepherd found on a battlefield became one of the biggest silent-film stars, and the fan mail that arrived for him was answered with a glossy portrait signed by a paw.

The credit read 'Played by himself', and the fan mail was answered by a paw. 1924 · Broadcast & Film

Close ×

The radio church of Sister Aimee

Her church went on the air on 6 February 1924: KFSG - 'Kall Foursquare Gospel' - broadcasting at 500 watts from Angelus Temple, listener-supported one day at a time. She is credited by her own church as the first woman to preach a sermon on radio (1922), and after the first broadcast the letters came from other states and other countries, cataloguing where the signal had been heard.

A church built to be heard at a distance. Aimee Semple McPherson had preached on Los Angeles radio before owning a station, and her church's history credits her as the first woman to preach a sermon over the radio, on the Rockridge station in Oakland in April 1922. In February 1924 KFSG went on the air from Angelus Temple - a 500-watt Western Electric transmitter whose dedication named its reach, 'the people of the United States, Canada, Alaska, Mexico, Panama, Hawaii, South Sea Islands'. The congregation had raised the roughly $25,000 to build it.

The letters were the reply channel, and they were the audience. In the early 1920s a station learned who was listening from the mail: cards, telegrams and letters reporting where the signal was received. The record kept for KFSG includes letters from Illinois, Indiana, Pennsylvania and New Zealand describing when the broadcast was heard and what it meant - 'We do hope to hear you soon again', one wrote, and a listener in Indiana asked who the woman preacher had been. The station answered its mail through volunteer church members, and it was sponsored day by day by listeners through the Radio Missionary Society, with patrons booked months ahead.

The mechanism is a congregational 'you' at a distance. The church addressed the radio congregation as assembled even though the far end could not answer: in Angelus Temple the pastor asked 'Do you pledge to live clean Christian lives for God?' and the answer - 'We do' - came back from a room the radio listeners could not join. That is the presenter mechanism with a congregation in it: second-person grammar aimed at an unseen audience, engineered on a schedule. The competing placement, the idol, fails because the sermons were live and ongoing - an address from somebody who believed herself perceived, not a finished body of work. One honesty note is kept: the famous 'minions of Satan' telegram to Secretary Hoover over a wavelength dispute is recorded by radio historians as legend rather than fact, and KFSG was never actually taken off the air.

The baseball the papers built

In 1927 Babe Ruth hit sixty home runs and became the most replayed figure in a game most fans knew through print. His bylined column ran in syndication, much of it ghostwritten, and radio was beginning to carry him into rooms the stadium never reached. The season the man and the persona arrived together. Ruth's 1927 season - sixty home runs in a 154-game schedule, a single-season record that stood until Roger Maris hit sixty-one in 1961, with the Yankees' Murderers' Row around him - is the on-field anchor this entry leans on without being about. The subject is the parallel figure the press built, because most of the people who loved him never saw him play.

The printed voice was another man's. Through the 1920s Ruth's byline ran across the country in syndicated columns and serials, and much of that copy was ghostwritten - the 'Babe Ruth' column was substantially the work of Christy Walsh, his agent, who organised the ghosted pieces under Ruth's name. A reader opening the sports page met a voice signed 'Babe Ruth' that Babe Ruth had not written, which is the manufactured reply end of a one-sided tie arriving in sports journalism: the attention felt answered, and the answer was an agency.

Radio and the syndicated page made the tie work at distance. Baseball went on the air in the 1920s, and Ruth's name travelled ahead of him by broadcast and by print - this entry records the 1920 film 'Headin' Home' as the star's celluloid echo of the same reach. The deeper claim, read from the thesis this entry draws on, is that the newspapers of the 1920s manufactured what one account calls a 'hot romance between sport and the public'. The counter-argument - that Ruth's fame was earned by a bat, not by columns - is real and kept on the entry; the point is narrower, that the relationship hundreds of thousands had with him ran through contributed copy and broadcast names rather than through a seat in a ballpark.

1938 · Broadcast & Film

Close ×

The War of the Worlds and the audience that believed the voice

Orson Welles's Mercury Theatre broadcast on 30 October 1938 presented H. G. Wells's invasion as a series of news bulletins, and some listeners took it as reportage.

The broadcast exploited the authority of the radio news voice: it interrupted dance music, used place names and interviewed supposed experts. The panic was real but much smaller than the legend; the audience research estimated that a minority of listeners were frightened and an even smaller number fled. The case matters because the mechanism is auditory trust, not credulity in general: a familiar voice in a familiar format can override what the listener knows.

The legend has since become a story about mass hysteria, which is itself a parasocial artefact - a public that enjoys believing it is uniquely suggestible. The documented broadcast is more interesting: most listeners changed the station, and the ones who stayed were often reassured by the very conventions that frightened others.

1940 · Politics & News

Close ×

This is London

The live rooftop broadcasts that carried the Blitz into American homes, and the sign-off that made a reporter and a city the same thing.

Every broadcast went out live, because the sound of the raid was the report. Murrow was, in Philip Seib's account, the only journalist allowed to broadcast live during the air raids on London - a permission arranged with Winston Churchill's intervention - and the recording technology of the day was, in Neal Conan's word and with Seib's agreement, klutzy; Murrow's argument was the silence and the anti-aircraft fire, transmitted while they were happening. The opening line, 'This is London', with its deliberate pause after This, was his speech teacher Ida Lou Anderson's suggestion, and it turned a city into a dateline. He closed with the farewell Londoners were already saying to each other in the blackout - 'Good night, and good luck'. On the roof of Broadcasting House he described the flags: 'No one ordered these people to put out the flags... No one told them to do it, and no flag up there was white.' Millions of Americans spent the autumn of 1940 in a city they had never visited, listening. The reply came later, at a dinner in December 1941. The Librarian of Congress, Archibald MacLeish, told Murrow what the broadcasts had done: 'You laid the dead of London at our doors and we knew that the dead were our dead, were mankind's dead. You have destroyed the superstition that what is done beyond 3,000 miles of water is not really done at all.' Nothing in the exchange was private; everything in it was personal. That is the whole subject of this site, worked out on a London rooftop, overnight radio, with the sky doing the sound effects.

Say, kids, what time is it?

The first television character to be answered by name. Children three to eight shouted its name back at the screen every afternoon for thirteen years, and the puppet it was played by was recorded before the broadcast.

The template for every children's presenter since, and the purest mechanism on this site: a figure that talks to you, built by somebody else, delivered from tape. 1956 · Broadcast & Film

Close ×

Horton and Wohl name the parasocial relationship

In 1956, Donald Horton and R. Richard Wohl published an account of the 'para-social relationship' between television viewers and the personalities who addressed them.

Horton and Wohl's contribution was to describe a structure rather than a pathology. The viewer is invited into a conversation with a performer who speaks to the camera as if to one person, and the viewer responds with the ordinary expectations of a relationship - attention, loyalty, a sense of knowing. The relationship is one-sided by design, not by failure.

They called it a 'simulacrum of conversational give and take'. The phrase is exact: the give and take is simulated on the performer's side, and the viewer's side is real. Almost every later argument about parasociality - whether it is healthy, exploitative, or simply a name for what audiences have always done - is a disagreement about which side of that sentence carries the weight.

'Stan': the fan who mistakes the reply for a promise

Eminem's 2000 song 'Stan' tells the story of an obsessive fan whose letters go unanswered until the rapper replies too late.

The song is built as a correspondence. Stan writes with the intimacy of someone who believes the artist owes him a response; the third verse is the artist's reply, arriving after Stan has driven into a river. The structural joke is that the reply is the one thing the fan needed, and it is also the one thing that cannot arrive in time.

The song gave the culture a name for the fan who cannot let go, and the name has since become a verb. Its accuracy is in the misreading: Stan treats the artist's songs as personal messages, which is exactly what a parasocial bond encourages. The artist's own voice is the evidence, and the evidence is one-way.

YouTube and the bedroom that looks at you

YouTube launched in 2005 and was bought by Google in 2006; the webcam vlog turned the domestic room into a broadcast studio.

The vlog's grammar is intimacy: a face close to the lens, a bedroom or kitchen behind it, direct address, and the opening 'hey guys'. The viewer is positioned as a friend who has dropped in. The production values are low on purpose - the low quality is evidence that this is not a performance, even when it is.

The medium also changed the scale of the far end. A television star was addressed to millions and could not see the reply; a vlogger could read comments and answer them in the next video, which made the relationship feel reciprocal without making it reciprocal. The reply is public, edited and addressed to everyone, but it arrives in the same room where the first video was watched.

Keeping Up with the Kardashians: the family as a schedule

Twitch and the donation that says your name

Twitch's live streaming and its donation and subscription tools turned watching into a real-time transaction in which the streamer can read the viewer's name aloud.

The streamer sees the chat, answers it, and reacts to donations in the moment. That is a genuine reply, which makes the relationship different from a television bond - but the reply is bought, and it is performed for an audience. The viewer is not a friend; the viewer is a name on a screen that the streamer is paid to acknowledge. By 2014, when Amazon acquired the service, the donation and subscription layer had become the platform's economic core.

The variable reinforcement is the mechanism: a donation may be read, may be ignored, may produce a shout-out or a joke. The uncertainty keeps the viewer watching and giving. Text-to-speech donations make the transaction audible to everyone, which turns the private wish to be noticed into a public event.

The podcast as an intimate medium

A voice in your ear, unedited, twice a week, for years. The most parasocial habit in the English language and rarely discussed as such.

Nobody wears a costume and nobody mentions the format, so it never got examined. Researchers eventually measured it and found listeners score high on exactly the traits predicted: novelty-seeking, curiosity, and a need for good conversation.

The medium's intimacy is technical: headphones, an unedited voice, a recurring schedule, and no face to correct the impression of a friend. The listener hears a person thinking in real time, which is a more convincing simulation of companionship than a scripted broadcast.

TikTok's For You page uses recommendation to assemble a personalised stream; by 2020 it had become the defining interface of short-form video.

The dead celebrity, generated

A voice trained on an archive, a likeness, a song, a speech, an appearance at a festival, all generated. Not a hologram, not a tribute, not a person.

The interesting fact is not that this happens without permission. Mostly it does not: the documented 2024 cases are licensed, one by an estate and one by the person himself while he was alive, which is a harder thing to argue with than the entry used to claim. Consent has moved to the licensor, and that is where the problem now lives. An estate agreeing is not the same as the dead person choosing how they will sound forever, and the sharpest case is the most defensible one - the actor who authorised his own recreation in advance, on purpose, because he was winding the role down and wanted the early performances preserved. Nobody was imposed on. Which is exactly why it is worth reading closely. Still unresolved, and not about the celebrities: the models are trained on tens of thousands of hours of audio scraped without consent, and the consent being bought covers the voice, not the training set.
