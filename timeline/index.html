Before 011

mostly 7 works and 4 mechanisms. filed as application.
  • 70000 BCThe dead kept in the caveA work

    Neanderthals repeatedly carried their dead into one cave in northern Iraq and put them down in the floor. Shanidar 4 was found on his side with his arm under his head; Shanidar Z lay in a hollow gouged out to fit her, with a stone under her head. The cluster dates to roughly seventy to seventy-five thousand years ago.

  • 40000 BCThe carved animalsA work

    Upper Paleolithic carvers in Europe made small figures of animals and people from mammoth ivory from about forty thousand years ago. The Vogelherd set includes a cave lion, a bison, a horse, a bear, a mammoth, and a lion-man - a body with a human face on a lion's. At La Madeleine in France a bison carved from antler is caught mid-grooming, turning its head to lick its own side.

  • 34000 BCBeads, and one bullA work

    At Sunghir on the Russian plain, thirty-four thousand years ago, three burials were lavish with beads and ochre and one was not treated that way at all. People were buried in a site that kept producing them for thousands of years, individually and very differently.

  • 15000 BCThe dogA mechanism or tactic

    A species that has spent fifteen thousand years being selected partly for forming attachment to one particular ape.

  • 9500 BCEleven thousand years of pillarsA work

    Round enclosures on a hillside in Anatolia holding T-shaped pillars carved to suggest human figures, built by people who had not yet domesticated a single animal - used for over a thousand years, then deliberately filled in and built over.

  • 800 BCThe story told againA work

    Myths are not stories people tell once. They are stories with a fixed shape that get told to the same audience on a schedule.

  • 660 BCThe craft of the sage AdapaA mechanism or tactic

    A tablet from Nineveh written in the voice of Ashurbanipal, listing what he had been taught and by whom - a named sage he never met - and then what he did with it, which was to put the tablets in his palace for the instruction of his subjects.

  • 370 BCShag-eared and deafA work

    Plato describes an older man in love with a boy who is compared, in the text, to a hunting animal that is deaf. The animal's ears are the point: the love is addressed to someone who cannot hear it, and the whole scene turns on that.

  • 250 BCThe wax masks in the hallA work

    A Roman aristocrat had a wax mask of his own face taken while he was alive - usually in his thirties or forties, on taking office - and it was kept in a cupboard in the entrance hall beside a painted family tree and a label listing his offices. He never saw it worn. After his death, actors put it on and walked it in his funeral procession, in order of death, wearing the robes of the office he had held.

  • 210 BCEight thousand faces, all facing outwardA mechanism or tactic

    Eight thousand life-size soldiers, each with a face and ears no other has, buried in battle order facing the enemy with the emperor behind them - made from the likenesses of real men so the substitute army would stand where the real one could not.

  • 200 BCThe congregationA mechanism or tactic

    A regular assembled audience for a figure who addresses them, in a building, on a schedule, for years.

it is a work. filed as application.
  • 787The icon answers nothingA work?

    In 787 the Second Council of Nicaea ended the first phase of the Byzantine iconoclastic dispute and defined what an icon was for: the honour paid to the image passes to the figure it shows. Icons were lawful because in Christ the invisible God had been seen. The panel itself never answered anyone.

1000-14994

mostly 2 works, people and mechanisms. filed as application.
  • 1150Ninety-three pieces, and a nameA work

    Walrus-ivory chess pieces from around 1150, buried on the Isle of Lewis and dug up in 1831, now divided between two museums and at least four incomplete sets. Two of them are probably replacements made in another workshop to replace pieces that had been lost.

  • 1373Margery KempeA person

    English woman who experienced what she described as an unbreakable bond with Jesus, was mocked for weeping in church, was reviled by her family, and in 1438 dictated a book about it that persuaded other women to do the same.

  • 1438BindingA mechanism or tactic

    Performing a self until the self holds, where it can be witnessed. Margery Kempe in white clothes in a world where widows wore black, weeping loudly in public for years, having decided she was going to be a specific kind of person.

  • 1438The Book of My First ShowingsA work

    A short prose book dictated by Margery Kempe describing her experiences of Christ's love, her suffering, and her certainty that she had been chosen.

1500-16994

mostly 2 people and 2 mechanisms. filed as application.
  • 1547Miguel de CervantesA person?

    Author of Don Quixote, and of a narrator who keeps having to explain away the reality of what his character is doing.

  • 1600The audienceA mechanism or tactic

    A room of people responding to a performance, learning the timing of the person on the stage.

  • 1605Don QuixoteA person

    An old man who reads so many books about chivalry that he goes out to live as a knight, against all evidence.

  • 1650Think of nothing but the guruA mechanism or tactic?

    Guru devotion asks a disciple to hold one teacher in mind without pause - moving, walking, sleeping, sitting, happy or miserable - and the Tibetan tradition makes it indispensable to the path rather than optional. The Gelug-pa school's principal guru-puja was compiled in the mid-seventeenth century, which is the date this entry carries; the practice it regularises is older than the book.

1700-17994

mostly 2 people, works and mechanisms. filed as application.
  • 1740Virtue rewardedA work?

    The novel that turned its readers into a correspondent. Pamela Andrews writes home about her master's pursuit, and the reader takes the seat of the parents - receiving every letter, sending none. Published anonymously in November 1740, it became the first English best-seller and the first novel whose readers wrote back demanding more of it.

  • 1749Johann Wolfgang von GoetheA person?

    Author of Werther, and of the observation that people keep making the same mistakes, which is arguably the first great novel to describe a reader being changed by a book.

  • 1758The wall exists to keep you outA mechanism or tactic?

    Diderot invented the wall in 1758 for the purpose of stopping the audience noticing. Every breach since - a wink, a clap, a lecture - is that convention failing on purpose, and it is the only time the far side admits you are there.

  • 1774WertherA person

    The young man of Goethe's novel who reads too much, loves a woman who does not love him back, and kills himself, in a book that made that behaviour fashionable across Europe for a decade.

1800-18998

mostly 4 people, 2 mechanisms and 2 works. filed as application.
  • 1812Charles DickensA person?

    Serialised fiction to a mass audience, weekly, to people who could not afford a book but could not afford not to read it.

  • 1861Miss HavishamA person

    A woman in Great Expectations who is left jilted at the altar, stops eating, ages, and arranges to be found the way she was on her wedding day - decades later, in a wedding dress, in a darkened room.

  • 1880Identification with a teamA mechanism or tactic

    Supporting a club as a condition of belonging to a place, a class, a family, or a city.

  • 1880The trading stampA mechanism or tactic

    A loyalty scheme by paper coupon, redeemable at any participating shop, popular decades before loyalty cards existed.

  • 1890Am I really like that?A work?

    The painter stands in front of the figure he made and asks whether the man is really like it. He is. And it will never alter, which is the only thing about it anybody wanted.

  • 1891The Divine SarahA person?

    Sarah Bernhardt toured more like a head of state than a player: when she sailed for New York in 1891 the crossing was covered the way a royal visit is, and 'Divine Sarah' was by then the whole public's phrase for her. Mark Twain's note put it exactly - there were five kinds of actresses, and Sarah Bernhardt. An idol built at distance.

  • 1897The comic stripA work?

    A recurring cast of characters, in a fixed panel order, appearing every single day without exception for decades.

  • 1899Alfred HitchcockA person?

    Director who said he made films about the tension between a man and the thing he cannot have.

1900-194941

mostly 14 works, 12 mechanisms and 9 people. filed as application.
  • 1900The coachA mechanism or tactic

    An adult with authority over a child for years, who is remembered partly by how they made them feel at nine.

  • 1900The landlordA mechanism or tactic

    A named individual with access to your home, your deposits and your address, met in person, repeatedly, under stress.

  • 1900The teacherA mechanism or tactic

    An authority figure seen daily, for years, who knows your name, your mood and your home situation.

  • 1900The transferA mechanism or tactic?

    A player who has been attached to for years leaves, and the grievance is felt as betrayal by the entire locality.

  • 1911Thousands came to gawk at the empty wallAn event or moment?

    An Italian workman walked out of the Louvre with the painting under his coat, and the wall it had hung on went on drawing a crowd: four iron hooks, no picture, and thousands of people come to look at the hooks anyway. The papers printed the absence, and when she came back she had become public property - the first mass art icon, made by a thief rather than by a painter.

  • 1911Thousands of letters, and a handful printedA mechanism or tactic?

    The first movie fan magazine appeared in February 1911, because by 1910 the letters were arriving faster than the studios could absorb them. What it invented was not the letter but the reply - a department, a set of rules, a Chicago address, and a flat refusal to publish studio addresses. Thousands of letters went in. A handful were printed.

  • 1912The film serialA work?

    Episodes released in instalments, each ending unresolved, the audience expected to return for the next one.

  • 1914A doodle of you used as your addressA person?

    By 1917 nine out of ten men at a costume ball came as him, a hold-up man in Cincinnati borrowed his costume as a disguise, and in 1916 somebody was paging him in more than eight hundred hotels at the same moment. Later, fans sent letters with a doodle of his face where the address should have been. The likeness had stopped being a picture and started being an instruction.

  • 1914Known to more people than any other woman in historyA person?

    The first film performer whose value could be counted instead of guessed. She was on the cover of the New York Dramatic Mirror in 1911, an honour then kept for theatrical stars; by 1916 her fan following had become ten thousand dollars a week, half her film profits and a production company of her own. The audience was never asked for any of it, and every line of the contract exists because of it.

  • 1920The athlete and the cityA mechanism or tactic

    Support for a team or an athlete as a bond with a person, a place and a lineage, frequently inherited from a parent.

  • 1920The fan mail departmentA mechanism or tactic?

    A physical object that had to be opened, a volume small enough for one person to hold, and a reply that was an exception. Clara Bow, Shirley Temple, Judy Garland, all had staff whose job was correspondence. Some of it came back.

  • 1921Cordially yours, Betty CrockerA person?

    In 1921 a flour company's advertising department needed one woman's name for the kitchen advice its all-female home-service staff was already writing. Betty Crocker was a signature and a persona, not a person: she answered letters, then took a radio voice in 1924, and gave millions a correspondent who signed herself "Cordially yours."

  • 1921They were not his, and they had never beenA person?

    A screen persona called the Latin Lover, and a death at thirty-one that produced public mourning on a scale nothing before it had managed - including a woman claiming an engagement he had never mentioned to anyone.

  • 1924The first fansAn event or moment

    The first documented fan clubs, organised around sheet music and radio rather than film. Following became joining.

  • 1924The radio church of Sister AimeeA person?

    Her church went on the air on 6 February 1924: KFSG - 'Kall Foursquare Gospel' - broadcasting at 500 watts from Angelus Temple, listener-supported one day at a time. She is credited by her own church as the first woman to preach a sermon on radio (1922), and after the first broadcast the letters came from other states and other countries, cataloguing where the signal had been heard.

  • 1927The baseball the papers builtA person?

    In 1927 Babe Ruth hit sixty home runs and became the most replayed figure in a game most fans knew through print. His bylined column ran in syndication, much of it ghostwritten, and radio was beginning to carry him into rooms the stadium never reached. The 1920s press manufactured a 'hot romance' and he was its star.

  • 1927The girl the studio builtA person?

    Paramount signed Clara Bow in 1926 and released six of her pictures in 1927, one of which - It - put a novelist's definition of 'It' onto a face and left the language with the phrase 'It Girl'. At the top of her fame she was the most-answered person in America: more than 45,000 fan letters arrived in January 1929 alone, and when fans found out she used henna, sales of the dye tripled.

  • 1928From two undelivered reels to a lunchboxA work?

    A cartoon mouse with a licensed lunchbox, a walk-of-fame star and a park where you can meet him - the first character to be manufactured as an object and then put back in the room with you.

  • 1928The audience organised against the showA work?

    Two white broadcasters voiced two fictional black men from March 1928, and at its peak about forty million listeners a night followed their fortunes - half the nation's radios, by one estimate. The audience the show loved most was also the one it caricatured, and from 1931 that audience organised.

  • 1930A magazine that did not existA work?

    Street & Smith hired two men to sell a magazine on the radio. They invented a voice instead, and the listeners went asking newsdealers for the magazine that voice implied.

  • 1930Radio and the soapA work?

    Broadcast drama on a fixed daily slot, written for an audience with nowhere else to be at that hour.

  • 1930Somebody always wrote backA work?

    Fifty-four years of letters to a fictional detective, answered by an office that had chosen not to have a face.

  • 1930The cliffhangerA mechanism or tactic

    End the episode before the answer. Give the viewer a fixed slot, a daily rhythm, and a question with no resolution, and they will come back tomorrow at the same time for years.

  • 1930Your face is too well knownA work?

    A dentist and a painter's sister were made into a national type on purpose, and one of them denied it for five years.

  • 1932Let alone, not aloneA person?

    In Grand Hotel (1932) Greta Garbo said 'I want to be alone', and the line became her biography: the press called her the Swedish Sphinx and MGM capitalised on the mystery. In LIFE in January 1955 she corrected the record - she had wanted to be let alone, not alone, and there is all the difference - but the correction did not take, and the letters she wrote at the time read lonelier than the legend does.

  • 1933As if he had joined them in their living roomsA mechanism or tactic?

    Franklin Roosevelt talked to the radio as if the listener were already in the room, and he did it often enough between March 1933 and June 1944 that the habit became a form - one he did not name. There is no solid definition of what counted as a chat; the estimates run from twenty-seven to thirty-one. The count does not matter. The sitting next to a fireplace does.

  • 1933Nineteen years of the version they were shownA work?

    A gorilla who feels affection for the woman offered to him, premiered to a city that had just had King Tut, was cut by censors, and did not get the missing scenes back until thirty-seven years later.

  • 1933The soap operaA work?

    A form built almost entirely by women, for women, and treated as unserious for fifty years. Daily episodes, fixed slots, cliffhangers, characters written in indefinitely and never resolved.

  • 1934Sixty-five percent by sevenA mechanism or tactic?

    A named, described character with an air of reality, kept going by being referred to. Two-thirds of children have one, most of them invisible, and a quarter of those are unknown to their parents.

  • 1938Nine to twelve million heard itAn event or moment?

    Orson Welles presented a novel as a news bulletin on 30 October 1938, and CBS identified it as a dramatic performance four times during the hour. An estimated nine to twelve million Americans heard it, and at least a million of them were scared into some sort of action. By the next morning the newspapers had a story about the listeners, which is the one that has been retold ever since.

  • 1938The first superheroA work?

    A character who cannot die, printed in a newspaper, read by a generation of children on the same day.

  • 1939The protagonist is a parasocial objectA term or concept?

    The first-person film, the subjective shot, the character who exists to be looked through rather than at.

  • 1940Entitled to the adulation of all lesser mortalsA mechanism or tactic?

    A children's comic club that awarded membership badges, and whose membership card was authorised by a character.

  • 1940The bobby-soxersAn event or moment?

    Teenagers organised in public, in letterpress newsletters, to buy records and argue about a singer. A press of ten thousand.

  • 1940Wishful thinkingA term or concept

    The long-standing explanation that parasocial feeling is a projection - people attach to a figure because the figure is available to be shaped by whoever is looking.

  • 1942I hope the feeling is mutualA work?

    Two years of letters to a listener who could not answer, written by a thirteen-year-old who said out loud that she hoped the feeling was mutual.

  • 1943GigiA work

    The song where the singer pleads to be given the exact shape a man wants: a French phrase, a café in the dark. A whole musical built around wanting to be a specific imaginary person for somebody.

  • 1945Not all ghosts are badA work?

    Made to answer a four-year-old's fear of the dark, by a man who made a cardboard cutout and hung it in a tree. Two men then claimed to have invented him.

  • 1945Registered by the applause meterA mechanism or tactic?

    A woman's misfortune told aloud, a crowd's reaction converted into a number, and the number chose her.

  • 1947Say, kids, what time is it?A work?

    The first television character to be answered by name. Children three to eight shouted its name back at the screen every afternoon for thirteen years, and the puppet it was played by was recorded before the broadcast.

  • 1948Nick DrakeA person?

    Made three records, withdrew from touring, and left behind an audience that argues about what he meant for fifty years.

  • 1950The local news anchorA mechanism or tactic?

  • 1950The neighbourA mechanism or tactic

  • 1950The retail clerkA mechanism or tactic

  • 1950The strip about unrequited loveA work?

A round-headed boy who loses everything, a dog who does not answer, a cartoon world that kept 350 million readers. Schulz maintained the loser's world on purpose.

  • 1955The Macy's counterA person

  • 1956Elvis PresleyA person?

  • 1956He did not ask to be buried as DraculaAn event or moment?

  • 1956Parasocial interactionA term or concept?

  • 1958Green Shield StampsA mechanism or tactic?

  • 1958The tape could not answerA work?

  • 1958VertigoA work

  • 1959The manufactured teen idolA mechanism or tactic?

  • 1960The political parasocialA mechanism or tactic?

  • 1960The sitcom familyA mechanism or tactic?

  • 1961The voice you distrustA term or concept?

    Wayne C. Booth coined unreliable narrator in The Rhetoric of Fiction in 1961: a narrator who does not speak for the norms of the work, judged against an implied author the reader constructs. Sixty years of argument later the term still describes the situation plainly - you are inside a voice you cannot verify, and nobody will correct you.

  • 1962Fifty copies of a faceA work?

    Warhol's 1962 double panel repeats one publicity still of Marilyn Monroe fifty times: colour on the left, draining to monochrome on the right, taken from Gene Kornman's Niagara photograph. Monroe died in August of that year, and the silkscreen turns her into a pattern a person can look at without her.

  • 1962The reply that was printedA mechanism or tactic?

    From 1962 Stan Lee ran fan mail in the back of The Fantastic Four and answered it in print in his own voice, so a reader could see their name answered on a page. The letters page became Marvel's institutional address to its readership, and its regular trophy, the No-Prize, was deliberately nothing.

  • 1963Dionne Warwick's fan mailA person?

    She received correspondence by the sackful and answered it by name, individually, for years. The closest thing the twentieth century produced to a genuinely reciprocal parasocial relationship.

  • 1964BeatlesmaniaAn event or moment?

    A global mass hysteria, documented live, with screaming, fainting, and an industry that began pricing it within months.

  • 1966ELIZAA mechanism or tactic?

    A short pattern-matching script that reflects the user's words back with questions attached. Most people told it they understood, within minutes.

  • 1966The dialogue programsA mechanism or tactic?

    ELIZA, PARRY and Colby - three programs that convinced people they were listening.

  • 1968Charisma as a measurable assetA study or paper?

    Research on why some political candidates reliably produce personal attachment and others do not, despite comparable policy positions.

  • 1968Won't you be my neighbor?A work?

    Fred Rogers went national on public television on 19 February 1968 and spent thirty-three years talking, slowly, into the camera at children aged two to five. The show's offer is mutual in its own words - "Won't you be my neighbor?" - and its host insisted he was not acting a character, only sharing his real self. The bond was engineered, and returned by people who could not answer.

  • 1970Characters move inA mechanism or tactic

  • 1970Grieving a person you never metA mechanism or tactic

  • 1970SHRDLUA mechanism or tactic?

  • 1971The figure you buyA mechanism or tactic?

  • 1971The first conventionAn event or moment?

  • 1973Elvis, in a hologram, from HawaiiAn event or moment?

  • 1976Convention cultureAn event or moment?

  • 1976The audience became the showAn event or moment?

  • 1976The finding that the satisfaction is not in the showA study or paper?

  • 1978The holiday specialA work?

  • 1979The uncanny corridor of companionshipA term or concept?

    The unsettling quality of a humanlike thing that is not human, and the narrow band where it is most compelling.

  • 1980The finale studyA study or paper?

  • 1980The season finaleA mechanism or tactic?

    Stories resolve on a fixed date, the wait is publicised in advance, and the audience is given a date to be in the same place at the same time.

  • 1981The distance programmeA mechanism or tactic?

  • 1982The supermarket clubA mechanism or tactic?

  • 1983M*A*S*HAn event or moment?

  • 1985Loneliness and local television newsA study or paper?

    A long-running body of work from Rubin and colleagues, repeatedly finding that people form parasocial bonds with news anchors, and that the strongest predictor of which anchor they bond with is not the anchor's performance.

  • 1985Parasocial interaction disorderA term or concept?

    A clinical name for a case where a one-sided bond is causing significant distress or impairment. Named in the eighties, and it is a real diagnosis for a small number of people.

  • 1985The counter sits betweenA mechanism or tactic

  • 1985Vote for the murdererA work?

The house was promised 1,200 possible endings and that it 'won't be rigged in any way'.

  • 1986So many people want to believe he's realA work?

  • 1988Robin died by 72 votesAn event or moment?

    In September 1988 Batman's readers called a premium-rate number, fifty cents a call, to decide whether the Joker's beaten Robin lived or died. The poll ran for about a day and a half and came down to 72 votes: 5,343 killed him, 5,271 spared him, and the issue that revealed the verdict had been prepared both ways.

  • 1990Is it good for you?A term or concept

  • 1990Mourning a person you metA term or concept?

  • 1990ShippingA mechanism or tactic?

    Fans insisting two characters should be together, and treating the show as withholding rather than as finished.

  • 1990The comfort rewatchA mechanism or tactic?

    The show you have already seen, watched again, not for the plot. Familiarity as a nervous system intervention.

  • 1990The stalker statuteA legal or documented case?

    Anti-stalking legislation passed in several jurisdictions, defining unwanted repeated contact as a criminal offence.

  • 1990The stamp cardA mechanism or tactic?

    Buy nine, get the tenth. A card, a punch, a visible record of loyalty, in the customer's hand.

  • 1991The NEST letterAn event or moment

  • 1992The confessional that became a conventionA mechanism or tactic?

    In 1992 MTV's The Real World gave reality television its least-questioned convention: a person alone in a room, speaking to a camera about what everyone just saw. The confessional was adopted by Survivor, Big Brother and the Housewives, and it endures because it is an interview dressed as a private thought, in a public space everyone pretends is private.
