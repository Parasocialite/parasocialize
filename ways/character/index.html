The sculptor who fell in love with an illusion, in 2026

A different Psychology Today piece, and the more useful of the two.

The word illusion does a lot of work in this genre and I dislike most of it, because it assumes the person on the other side is doing the illusioning. Sometimes the illusion is entirely self-generated, and nobody at the other end is performing anything at all. That is the version nobody writes about, and it is the version a lot of people are living.

  • Entitled to the adulation of all lesser mortals1940 · A mechanism or tacticFiled character/fictional-protagonist - "somebody described in a book and therefore available to an unlimited number of people at once" - which is where nancy-drew also sits, and the pairing is deliberate: one written character reached by letter for fifty-four years, one reached by club membership. Captain Marvel was reached by a code printed in the book the reader was holding, which is the same unlimited-addressability with a transaction bolted to it. other: made, and this is the case that proves the nancy-drew hole is a hole rather than a misfiling. made requires the operator to be visible - "the transparency is a design choice", the audience can see the person operating the thing. Here they can: C. C. Beck drew the cover, Bill Parker wrote the script, Wilford H. Fawcett was the executive editor, and all three are credited on the book. nancy-drew's ghostwriter was hidden behind a house pseudonym for 44 years and made correctly refuses it. Same category, one with the byline and one without - so the axis's not clause is doing real work and item 40's missing case is a genuine gap rather than a misfiling. That distinction matters, because it is the difference between "the axis is wrong" and "the axis has one state it does not have", and only the second is worth adding a member for. rec: simulated, and this is now the fourth simulated entry and the least like the other three. live-commentary and twitch-subs work by latency - a host reads a comment aloud within seconds. nancy-drew works by post - a correspondence department, weeks apart. This one is a physical artefact: a membership card, a certificate, a plastic ring, issued to a child who decoded the message himself. It is also the only one where the performer of the reciprocity is named on the artefact - "issued under the actual authority of Benj J. Grimm, Grand Marshal (pro tem)", a character, signing for an institution. The Dell club's certificate two doors down was authorised by G.T. Delacorte Jr., an actual person. Two neighbouring children's clubs made the same promise and one of them kept the human out of it, and that contrast is the evidence that the signature was a choice rather than a constraint. And this is the entry that gives the cog register a named instance. The register asks whether seeing the whole machine would change the attachment - "if seeing it changes nothing about the attachment, it is Cog" - and that test is satisfied here by the cheapest possible evidence: the cipher was printed in the comic the reader was holding. A reader who knows exactly how the private channel works is not thereby a different kind of reader. "A cog that can see the machine is still a cog." The far end has its own machinery (a premium scheme - "a color picture of Spy Smasher for a quarter"), every member has their own (the decoding), and no child in any of the four adjacent clubs built any of them. Until this entry the register had a definition, a test, a not and no instance, which is the same state undemanded was in before dear-kitty, and a register with nothing behind it prints on the taxonomy page as though it were a finding.
  • Somebody always wrote back1930 · A workFiled character/fictional-protagonist because that shelf's definition is almost a transcription of the case: "Somebody described in a book and therefore available to an unlimited number of people at once". Nancy Drew is the unlimited part - a printed series addressed to children the Syndicate did not know and could not count, and fifty-four years of it. companion/social-agent was the other candidate and is wrong: that shelf is "a streamer-shaped entity with a persistent identity, a viewer count and an audience that gathers", and nobody in 1930 could see a viewer count. The object of the tie is a written character, not a system on a platform. rec: simulated, and the value is doing real work here rather than being decorative. A reply genuinely arrived - letters were answered, and the collection holds audio letters and commercial dramatic readings of the series - so the tie was not merely one-sided in appearance. What the axis asks is whether anything was felt on the far side, and the answer is a departmental process with no interior: "Carolyn Keene" was a slot in a production system reused across series by writers who never met. This is the first simulated instance that is neither live nor digital. live-commentary and twitch-subs both work by latency - a host reads a comment aloud within seconds. A correspondence department works by post: the distance between a child's letter and its reply is a postal system, and the answer was typed on Syndicate stationery. Same structure, and a scale the other two do not have. Deliberately left unplaced on otherness, and the axis has a hole here. imagined requires the audience to know they made the thing - "does the audience know they made it? If yes and nothing else in the world supports it" - and the readers did not know, with thirty-odd published books standing behind the character. made requires the operator to be shown, and its own not sends the hidden case to played-role, which is a different thing again. Mildred Wirt Benson wrote 23 of the first 30 books under a house pseudonym that was not a person, and her identity was not made public until 1991 - so for the entire life of the fan-mail subseries the figure was operated by somebody the audience was never shown and never knew about. That is a fourth state the axis does not have: a figure written by an operator the audience is not shown. the-shadow and howdy-doody are made with the transparency intact, because a voice actor, an advertising agency and a writer-director are all discoverable; this is the same machinery with the byline bolted shut. Leaving the field empty and saying so here is the honest move - adding a ninth otherness member is an editorial decision, and it belongs to the author rather than to a script that happened to find an entry which needs one.
  • Virtue rewarded1740 · A workFiled character/fictional-protagonist, on the shelf's test: "Is the figure available in more than one place at the same time, to people who have never met?" - and the second half of that test is what settles it, "if no author can override what the audience says they feel". Pamela is the rare case where the author tried and lost: the entry records that "The audience's want bent the text." - the unauthorized continuations and parodies "drew a sequel from Richardson himself, Pamela in her Exalted Condition, in 1742". The def fits the form rather than the sales, "Somebody described in a book and therefore available to an unlimited number of people at once, none of whom can be contradicted", and the mechanism's first clause is the entry's whole structure, "A fictional interior is an invitation with no host present": the letters "are written not to a lover but to Pamela's parents, who are offstage, at a distance, and never answer inside the book", which hands the reader "the seat of the parents - receiving every letter, sending none". The counter is character/mascot, and it is real because the reception left objects behind - Pamela motifs on teacups and fans are a shop-shelf reading of the tie. It loses on that shelf's own test, "Does the figure have an interior anybody could get wrong, or is it specified entirely by others?" - the audience spent the decade disagreeing about her interior, dividing into "Pamelists and Antipamelists", and a figure a crowd can be wrong about is exactly the one "with enough interior life that the audience fills it in and cannot be corrected" - the mascot's not stated positively. The second counter is persona/creator, since the letters out of the book went to Richardson. It loses because what the book manufactures is "a one-sided correspondence the reader reads from the parents' end", and Richardson's sequel was an answer to Pamela's public rather than a creator's output being followed. Contestable, and recorded as such.
  • The strip about unrequited love1950 · A workFiled character/fictional-protagonist, on the shelf's test: "Is the figure available in more than one place at the same time, to people who have never met?" Charlie Brown was in hundreds of newspapers at once, read by readers who had never met any of each other, and the strip's own argument is that the unrequited love is the figure's interior - "the reader fills it in and cannot be corrected", which is the shelf's mechanism and the entry's point about a character defined by a repeated failure to be answered. The counter is persona/idol: a made figure at a distance, image maintained by others. It loses on the idol's own not, which says "Not a fiction" - and the attachment here is unambiguously to a fiction, one whose specific claim (unrequited love as the shape of the strip) only a written character can carry, because a performer offers a version of themselves while Charlie Brown has no performer to leave the costume. The second half of the shelf's test is what separates him from a controlled property: Schulz died in 2000 and the audience's picture of Charlie Brown was not overridable by the estate's continuity strips, which is the pointed end of the entry.

  • The voice you distrust1961 · A term or conceptFiled character/fictional-protagonist, on the shelf's test: "Is the figure available in more than one place at the same time, to people who have never met?" A narrator is available to every reader holding the book at once and to none of them in person, which is the shelf's def exactly: "Somebody described in a book and therefore available to an unlimited number of people at once, none of whom can be contradicted." The entry's own condition is the mechanism's first half, "A fictional interior is an invitation with no host present", sharpened by the summary into what the reader actually stands in: "you are inside a voice you cannot verify, and nobody will correct you." Booth's test puts the verdict where the shelf puts it - the norms are "the implied author's norms", an entity nobody on the page can produce - so "it is a verdict reconstructed from the shape of the telling, by a reader standing alone with it", and the text never closes the account: sixty years on the term is still "coined once, revised by its coiner, extended by everyone else", which is the audience-supplied continuity the shelf describes running faster than any author can correct it. The counter is character/played-role, and it takes the strongest form available on this site: The Narrator is played on screen and the split is visible, with "The Narrator, an unreliable narrator, is not immediately aware that he is mentally projecting Tyler" as the case an audience holds. It loses on that shelf's own not, "The played role requires the performer to be legible as a person" - the term needs no performer at all, since a narrator in a novel has none, and where there is one the unreliability does not sit in him: Booth's definition decides against norms the actor cannot play, and the corpus records the mechanism as "An unreliable narrator is a person who knows and maintains anyway", a state of knowing held by a voice rather than by a casting choice. Contestable, and recorded as such.

The mascot 0 stories · 6 entries

A figure with no body, no biography and no intention: an emote, a logo-person, a face given a name. The purest parasocial object on the site, because nothing can disappoint it.

  • Elvis, in a hologram, from Hawaii1973 · An event or momentFilmed in a two-dimensional process that placed an apparently three-dimensional Elvis on stage, in a scene where real people were supposed to react to him. A likeness with no continuity, reaching half the planet - the archive register performed as technology.
  • Trying to live up to the standard of his girls1890 · A workSpecified entirely by a pen and the pages that bought it: there is no interior anybody could get wrong, because there is no interior at all - the test's first half answers itself, and 'can it be the author of its own behaviour' is answered by the medium. That incapacity is the point of the attachment: a drawing cannot contradict the thousands of girls living up to it, cannot be caught failing, and cannot be disappointed by them either. Not a written character, who could at least talk back on the page.

The historic as character 0 stories · 3 entries

A real person who has been flattened into a type - the long-suffering husband, the tragic wife, the mad king - and is now met in the type rather than in the life. The flattening is the problem, not the person.

  • Your face is too well known1930 · A workFiled character/historic-as-character - "a real person who has been flattened into a type" - and the placement is contestable, so it is recorded as contestable. The honest counter-argument: the tie is to the painted pair, who are not real people, and the object is a painting available to unlimited viewers at once, which is fictional-protagonist's shape. historic-as-character wins because Wood said outright that the pair were "types of people I have known all my life" and deliberately stretched a real face until it could not be recognised - the flattening is documented and intended, which is that shelf's whole definition. But an entry about two models flattened into a national type sits close enough to the boundary that the alternative is defensible. This is the placement to revisit if the axis grows. other: made, and this one genuinely fits rather than being a compromise. made is "an object with a face, a voice or an operator: a puppet, a doll, an automaton, a character who is performed by somebody the audience can see if they look", and its test is "is somebody working it?". Wood is continuously, publicly operating the thing. He modelled his dentist on a chair in a dental office, costumed both figures from a Sears mail-order order, painted his sister's face long on purpose so she could not be recognised, and then for the rest of his career posed for photographs in overalls identical to the figure's. The transparency condition made requires - the audience can see the person operating the thing - holds here, and it is the whole entry. And this is what makes it the control for krapp-last-tape rather than a third unplaceable case. Both are a figure with a person behind it. In american-gothic the person behind is visible and stays visible, which is what made is for. In krapp-last-tape the person behind is a recording thirty years old and four words short, and the audience is never shown him - so made's mechanism refuses it, since it presumes the figure answers. Same category, opposite conditions, and the axis has a word for exactly one of them. That is the strongest form of the argument that a ninth member is needed, and it is now demonstrable from two adjacent entries rather than asserted. Reciprocity left unset. The condition here is directed, which is the corpus default. Worth noting that undemanded is also defensible - nothing was asked of the painted pair and the tie is one-sided - and is not set for the reason the entry itself gives: Wood drew a "storm of protest from Iowa farm wives", one of whom threatened to smash her head, and the dentist denied the tie for five years. Undemanded is about nobody wanting anything; it is not about nobody objecting.
  • Ninety-three pieces, and a name1150 · A workCarved mythological types rather than portraits, so the face you attach to was an idea made in walrus ivory - and at least two of them are replacements, from another workshop, that nobody can identify on sight. The clearest case on this site of a bond forming on an object in full view of the people who keep it.
  • Fifty copies of a face1962 · A workFiled character/historic-as-character, on the shelf's test: "Do you know the type, and would the real biography survive contact with it?" The painted Marilyn is a type imposed on a dead woman - fifty copies of one publicity still, drained of colour - encountered through a role the silkscreen assigned after her death, and every accurate fact about the person is in tension with the pattern, which deletes the biography rather than surviving contact with it. The shelf's not speaks the entry's sentence: "the person existed and the type was imposed, so every accurate detail is in tension with the attachment." The counter is persona/idol, where this corpus filed the empty-wall painting - a public image the person is not required to maintain. It loses on the shelf's own distinction: an idol is encountered through the person's image however distant, whereas here the manufacture went on after the person died and the imposition of the type is the subject rather than an incident. The deeper contest is companion/object - attachment to a thing with no agency at all - whose own note records that it is empty; fifty identical faces brush against it, but the object here is an image of someone, which is the historic-as-character test, not the object test. Contestable, and recorded as such.
