Top 8: The Voice in Your Head

The oldest mass-production of a person. Ranked by how much of the idol was built by the audience rather than the artist.

Music is where the parasocial bond learned to scale: a record travels without the performer, a tour sells proximity, and a death turns the catalogue into an estate. The eight below are the mechanism at different stages, from a fan club to a posthumous release.

Read this before the list

The eight are drawn from entries already in the record; each links to its own page and that page's sources. 8 of the 8 have no photograph, so their entries carry text only. The absence is part of the record.

LAW 1 Every entry here is an instance of Binding is public, which is the reason they are on one list.

  1. 1

    A letter in words and music

    Music / Radio · 1941

    LAW 1 · Binding is public

    What happens. A radio programme addressed the men of the forces as a letter in words and music, and the voice behind it kept the address running for the rest of her life.

    Why it is on the list. An institution pointed a microphone at a nation at war and put one voice behind it. Vera Lynn's wartime programme, Sincerely Yours, went out on 9 November 1941 at half past nine, and it was described as 'to the men of the forces - a letter in words and Music' - a phrase that reads like a job description for this site's whole subject.

  2. 2

    Kurt Cobain

    Music · 1994

    LAW 1 · Binding is public

    What happens. A death broadcast in real time to a generation that had grown up with a songwriter's complaints as a soundtrack.

    Why it is on the list. Parasocial grief with a specific edge: the person had spent years being public about being miserable, and the audience had been treating it as content. Afterwards, every public complaint became a private grief waiting to happen.

  3. 3

    The band played without him

    Music · 1944

    LAW 1 · Binding is public

    What happens. The most popular bandleader in America gave up the job to enlist, took his orchestra to England, and vanished over the Channel on a small plane to Paris - while the band played on.

    Why it is on the list. He was at the top of the business, and he gave the business up to put on a uniform.

  4. 4

    Prince

    Music · 2016

    LAW 1 · Binding is public

    What happens. A death announced through an initialism, and a global response before anybody knew the details.

    Why it is on the list. Grief engineered to announce itself, and the parasocial bond used deliberately as a broadcast channel by the person who had it.

  5. 5

    Nick Drake

    Music · 1948–1974

    LAW 1 · Binding is public

    What happens. Made three records, withdrew from touring, and left behind an audience that argues about what he meant for fifty years.

    Why it is on the list. The clearest case of the bond forming after the person withdrew from it, and of the work becoming the only place the relationship could continue.

  6. 6

    The crowd injury

    Music · 2005

    LAW 1 · Binding is public

    What happens. Fans are injured or killed attempting to get closer to a performer, in volume, repeatedly, for years.

    Why it is on the list. Direct physical risk from proximity-seeking, and the clearest line where attachment stops being a private matter and becomes somebody else's liability.

    Read the entry →

  7. 7

    Gigi

    Film · 1943

    LAW 1 · Binding is public

    What happens. The song where the singer pleads to be given the exact shape a man wants: a French phrase, a café in the dark. A whole musical built around wanting to be a specific imaginary person for somebody.

    Why it is on the list. The template for a large fraction of the twentieth century. Cole Porter did not invent the impulse, he published it, and the song is still the most precise description of the thing that every subsequent song about obsessive attachment is a worse version of.

    Read the entry →

  8. 8

    The podcast as an intimate medium

    Music · 2014

    LAW 1 · Binding is public

    What happens. A voice in your ear, unedited, twice a week, for years. The most parasocial habit in the English language and rarely discussed as such.

    Why it is on the list. Nobody wears a costume and nobody mentions the format, so it never got examined. Researchers eventually measured it and found listeners score high on exactly the traits predicted: novelty-seeking, curiosity, and a need for good conversation.
