## 2026-10-03 — the fourth wall, and three instances that use it

Fetched and read in full: Wikipedia `Fourth_wall`, `Harvey_(1950_film)`,
`Harvey_(play)`, `Fight_Club`, `Deadpool_(film)`, plus the *Los Angeles Times*
ALF piece and Project Gutenberg's `pg174.txt` (Dorian Gray) in earlier batches.
`AFI` catalog `moviedetails/50671` returned **the wrong film** (*The Stooke*,
1953) and TCM returned nav chrome only — neither is citable.

### Fourth wall — the etymology, which is the good part

- The concept is **attributed to Denis Diderot, 1758**: actors and writers
  should "imagine a huge wall across the front of the stage, separating you
  from the audience, and behave exactly as if the curtain had never risen."
  Note what the wall is *for*: the convention exists to keep the audience out.
- **Diderot's "un grand mur" became "the fourth wall" to Leigh Hunt in 1807**,
  writing about the actor Mr. Bannister: "No actor enters so well into the
  spirit of his audience as well as his author, for he engages your attention
  immediately by seeming to care nothing about you; the stage appears to be his
  own room, of which the audience compose the fourth wall."
- **February 1879**, Henry Irving, on a stage device for *Hamlet*: "It should
  never be forgotten that the stage has four walls, though the fourth is only
  theoretical." The **first known use of "fourth wall theory"** follows later
  that month, in a comment on that article in the British periodical *Truth*.
- **Vincent Canby, 1987**: "that invisible scrim that forever separates the
  audience from the stage."
- Rejected: I could not source a clean coinage year. The article gives the
  concept 1758, the phrase 1807, and the term "fourth wall theory" 1879, and
  attributing the coinage to any one of them would be picking whichever suits.
  **Entry is dated 1758 to the concept and says so in the text.**

### The instances, quoted

- **Peter Pan** appeals to the audience to revive Tinker Bell: *"If you believe
  in fairies, clap your hands!"* Breaking the wall here is **an instruction the
  audience obeys**, and the fiction survives only if they do. Pantomime and
  children's theatre are named as the places where this is most common.
- **Mary MacLane**, *Men Who Have Made Love to Me* (1918): "one of the earliest
  recorded breakings of the fourth wall in serious cinema", the author
  portraying herself and interrupting the vignettes to address the audience.
- **Reefer Madness** (1936), Josef Forte as Dr. Alfred Carroll, breaks it at the
  end to warn viewers. **Groucho Marx** in *Animal Crackers* (1930) and *Horse
  Feathers* (1932), the latter advising viewers to "go out to the lobby".
- **Bugs Bunny and other Warner Bros. characters "regularly broke the fourth
  wall"**, per the article — Merrie Melodies shorts *Daffy Duck and Egghead*
  (1938) and *Thugs with Dirty Mugs* (1939), both directed by Tex Avery, also
  featuring gags with characters representing audience members. **This is the
  user's "bunny talking directly to the audience"** — they asked about a rabbit
  and the documented instance is this one, not a character named Harvey.
- **Woody Allen**, *Annie Hall* (1977), on why: "I felt many of the people in
  the audience had the same feelings and the same problems. I wanted to talk to
  them directly and confront them." *The Purple Rose of Cairo* (1985) makes it
  a plot point. **Jerry Lewis**, *The Total Filmmaker* (1971): "Some
  film-makers believe you should never have an actor look directly into the
  camera… I think that is nonsense, and usually I have my actors, in a single,
  look direct into the camera at least once in a film, if a point is to be
  served."
- **The honesty note that matters most**, and the reason this cannot be a
  sub-category: in mockumentary, "because the camera crew and interview
  situation may be part of the fictional documentary frame, looking at or
  speaking to the camera does not always function as a convention." George
  Burns broke it on *The George Burns and Gracie Allen Show* (1950); *The
  Office* is named as the modern form.

### Harvey (1950)

- Directed by **Henry Koster** — *not* Cukor, which was my assumption before
  checking. Stars James Stewart, Josephine Hull (Veta), Charles Drake, Cecil
  Kellaway (Dr. Chumley), Jesse White. Based on Mary Chase's 1944 play.
- Harvey is "a 6 ft 3 + 1/2 in tall white invisible rabbit", described by Elwood
  as a **púca**. The play spells it **pookah** (p-o-o-k-a), and the character
  Wilson reads the definition from a dictionary on page 33 of Chase's playbook.
- Play: **1944**, premiered **1 November 1944** at the 48th Street Theatre;
  **Pulitzer Prize for Drama awarded 1945**. The film article says "the 1944
  play"; a disambiguation page also gives 1944. Do not write 1946.
- Wikipedia's own neutral summary hedges: Elwood "claims to have an unseen
  (and presumably imaginary) friend". **An encyclopedia hedging is worth
  quoting**, and it is the honest register for the entry.
- **The plot is the thesis.** Veta, his sister, "in exasperation, admits to the
  attending psychiatrist, Dr. Sanderson, that she sees Harvey once in a while
  herself" — and is committed in Elwood's place. Dr. Chumley, who cannot see
  Harvey either, "wandered off with Harvey after several rounds of drinks."
  **The relationship survives because the people around it play along**, and
  two professionals who should have diagnosed it end up talking to the rabbit.

### Fight Club (1999)

- Fincher; Norton, Pitt, Bonham Carter. From Palahniuk's 1996 novel. Shot
  July–December 1998. **Studio executives did not like it** and restructured
  the marketing to cut expected losses. Gross $102.4M.
- The load-bearing sentence: **"The Narrator, an unreliable narrator, is not
  immediately aware that he is mentally projecting Tyler."**
- Norton's own account, which is the physical evidence: "We decided early on
  that I would start to starve myself as the film went on, while Brad Pitt
  would lift and go to tanning beds; he would become more and more idealized as
  I wasted away."
- Why `mirrored` and not something softer: Dorian Gray's split is between an
  image and a man; this split is **inside one voice**. The mechanism that hides
  it — the unreliable narrator — is the same device as the imaginary companion:
  the person knows, and maintains it anyway.

### Deadpool (2016)

- Tim Miller, feature debut. Reynolds. Marvel Comics character Wade Wilson.
  Greenlit 2014 after test footage leaked; budget $58M, gross $782.6M, 108 min.
- **The fourth-wall breaking is a property of the comics, not a film
  invention**: the spinoff "was set to ignore the Wolverine version of Deadpool
  and return to the character's roots with a slapstick tone and a 'propensity
  to break the fourth wall'". Fabricated, Danny Minahan and Rhett Reese & Paul
  Wernick are named in the same passage as having "got it right off the bat".
- The economics are the argument: an R-rated character who talks to you, on a
  $58M budget, grossed $782.6M. **The address is the marketing.**

## 2026-10-03 — rejected: the fourth wall is NOT always the fourth wall

The boundary that keeps this from being a sub-category, recorded because it is
the thing most likely to be got wrong: in mockumentary the camera crew may be
*inside the fiction*, so looking at camera is not a breach at all. Same shape as
the cull audit's in-fiction-response exemption. **A convention only counts if
the audience knows it is a convention.**

### The real blocker on the 63 entries with no locator is not the DOIs

Twenty-four of those entries were investigated for a locator. The finding is that
**the DOI was never the missing piece — the citation is too thin to verify one
against.**

| citation in the corpus | what a locator search needs |
|---|---|
| `Rubin, A. M., Perse, E. M., McVoy, S. M., & Brannan, D. (1985).` | a title. There is none. |
| `Jussila et al. (2020).` | a title. There is none. |
| `Higgs & McInstash (2008).` | a title. There is none. |
| `de Vries, L., & Gilovich, T. (2015). **Title and venue not yet recorded.**` | the corpus says so itself |

So `verify_doi.py --find` was run against eleven of them with author-plus-title
queries, and the honest result was **0 verified** — not because no DOI exists,
but because a candidate cannot be told apart from its neighbours. The searches
did find the right works in some cases; the point is that nothing in the corpus
distinguishes them from the wrong ones.

**And the attempt to relax the check made that concrete.** Allowing author+year
to be sufficient produced an immediate false positive: the corpus cites
`Jussila et al. (2020)` with no title, and the search returned
`10.1007/978-981-15-0069-5_13` — *"Sensory Technologies for Improving Employee
Experience and Strengthening Customer Loyalty"* — a different paper, same first
author, same year, matched and accepted. **An author and a year are a pair of
coordinates, not a work.** Any prolific author has dozens of papers in any given
year, so the pair narrows a search and identifies nothing in it. It is enough to
make a candidate worth reading. It is not enough to write down as a locator, and
`verify_doi.py` now refuses to return MATCH without title evidence.

`parasocial-persuasion` is the clearest case in the corpus of the whole
problem: its citation field literally reads *"Title and venue not yet
recorded."* That is an honest admission and it is also the whole diagnosis — the
entry is not missing a URL, it is missing the information a URL would be checked
against.

**The order of work therefore matters more than the searching.** Completing the
citation first — author, year, title, venue — makes the locator mechanically
checkable. Searching first produces candidates that cannot be confirmed, and a
candidate that cannot be confirmed is how `kind: exact` happened.

## 2026-10-03 — DOI identity audit: 5 real discrepancies, 3 that were the tool's fault

`tools/verify_doi.py --from-citations` checked all 44 DOI-bearing records against Crossref by **author and year**, not by whether the DOI loads. Results: 25 MATCH, 8 MISMATCH, 5 UNCHECKED.

**A resolving DOI is not evidence of identity.** Crossref's bibliographic query is a fuzzy search that always returns something: asked for thirteen titles it returned a DOI for all thirteen, and two were the work being looked for. One of the eleven that were not resolved `10.64628/aa.ketwakysr`, **"Man's stressed friend: how your mental health can affect your dog"** — for a citation about parasocial persuasion. Attaching that DOI would have looked like verification and been a lie.

**Three of the eight mismatches were the tool's fault, and reading them by hand is the only reason that is known:**

- Crossref stores one author as `C<t>` — mojibake for **Côté**, so a correct DOI read as a mismatch on a name comparison.
- Two corpus citations **begin with the title and carry no author** (`Avatars are (sometimes) people too...`, `Stoking the Flames: ...`), so the surname extractor read the first word as a family name. The DOIs are correct; the citations are incomplete, which is its own problem and is listed below.

**Five are real and are recorded rather than fixed**, because each is a choice between a wrong date and a wrong locator, and the corpus does not contain enough to decide:

| entry | DOI | corpus says | DOI registers | 
|---|---|---|---|
| `merten-identification` | `10.1108/SBM-05-2023-0063` | 2020 | 2023 |

  - **Same authors, near-identical title: 'Fan identification in football: professional football players and clubs competing for fans'. The DOI resolves to the 2023 version. Either the corpus year is wrong, or a 2020 version exists and the locator points at a later one.**
| `psr-grief-kutner` | `10.1177/0030222815600450` | 2018 | 2015 |

  - **'R.I.P. Kutner: Parasocial Grief Following the Death of a Television Character'. The DOI is registered to 2015. The corpus year may be a later print or a different item.**
| `klastrup-facebook-mourning` | `10.1080/13614568.2014.983564` | 2015 | 2014 |

  - **'I didn't know her, but': parasocial mourning of mediated deaths on Facebook RIP pages. The DOI is registered to 2014; a 2015 print or another version is plausible.**
| `narrative-brands-end` | `10.1086/673959` | 2013 | 2014 |

  - **'When Narrative Brands End: The Impact of Narrative Closure and Consumption Sociality'. The DOI is registered to 2014.**
| `subscription-streams` | `10.1002/cb.2369` | Alba | Banerji, Gupta & Ketron (2024) |

  - **'Navigating consumption streams: Toward a utilitarian-hedonic perspective on subscription consumption'. **The named author does not appear among the DOI's authors at all.** Either the citation names the wrong person or the locator points at a different paper with the same title. This is the only one of the five where the work itself may be wrong, rather than the date.**

Not one of these was corrected, because correcting either end would mean choosing between "the year is wrong" and "the locator is wrong" without evidence, and this corpus does not invent the smaller of two uncertainties to make the page tidy. **They are a work queue of five, and the fifth is the only one where the cited work itself may be wrong.**

Two further citation-quality problems the audit exposed, both benign but both real: citations that carry a **title and no author**, and citations whose title includes the journal's own metadata (`11K Shopping Websites` where Crossref truncates to `Dark Patterns at Scale`, or `245-268` where the title is `On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots`). The latter is why `verify_doi.py` treats an author-and-year agreement as decisive and the title as corroborating: a citation and a registry routinely disagree about titles for reasons that are not errors.

### What the tool got wrong on the way, in order

1. It compared the **whole citation** — eight authors, a journal, a volume and the words `Article 81` — against a bare title. All 44 records came back MISMATCH, including `10.1145/3359183`, which is *Dark Patterns at Scale* by Mathur, Acar and Friedman, exactly what `loyalty-dark-pattern` cites. **Zero of forty-four is not a plausible result**, which is the only reason it was caught.
2. It compared surnames by trailing token, so an author Crossref stores given-name-first (`family: "Arunesh"` for Mathur Arunesh) compared against a given name. Now every token of every name is tried.
3. It let a title mismatch **override** a matching author and year, so a citation carrying a subtitle that Crossref truncates read as a different paper. Author and year now decide; the title corroborates.

Each of those is the same defect the site keeps meeting in another place: a measurement that is confidently reporting on the wrong layer.
