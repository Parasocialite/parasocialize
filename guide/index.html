BEFORE WE START

Why I wrote this down

I grew up in Downers Grove, Illinois, in a house where the TV was always a little too loud. Then I spent my twenties managing Blockbuster stores across three states, which means I spent my twenties learning what people watch when they're lonely, heartbroken, bored, or fourteen. People told their clerk things they'd never tell their friends. I never forgot a single rental history I shouldn't have memorized.

Later I earned an M.A. and wrote about Margery Kempe — a medieval woman who made her inner life spectacularly public, white clothes and loud weeping and all. Later still I landed in classrooms in Australia, where I tell teenagers that the feelings they have about people on their phones are old feelings wearing new clothes. And in between I wrote novels about an industry that records people's dreams, because I couldn't stop thinking about who gets to hold the things we feel most privately.

This guide is everything those rooms taught me, boiled down to seven patterns I actually use. Not to diagnose you. Not to scold you. To give you language — the way a good clerk gives you the right tape on a Friday night. Fifteen minutes a pattern. A worksheet at the end. Take what helps, leave the rest on the shelf.

HOW TO USE THIS

Read straight through once, then keep it where your phone charges at night. When some attachment starts costing you sleep, money, or peace, flip to the worksheet, find the pattern, and ask the one question at the bottom of each law. That's the whole method. Notice, name, choose.

Law 1 — Binding is public

FROM FILE 001 · KEMPE → STANS

Margery Kempe wore white in a world where widows wore black. She wept in churches where ladies stayed composed. Every choice said: this is who I am, and I'm saying it where you can see me. My thesis called it binding — performing the self until the self holds.

Now look at a stan header. The layout, the bracelet emoji, the streaming streak number, the anniversary counter on a paid message app. Same work, new pixels. None of it is private, and that's the point. Binding only binds if someone witnesses it. A playlist nobody sees is taste. A playlist in your bio is membership.

I'm not above this. My Blockbuster nametag bound me — Enan, movies, ask me anything — and I wore it like white clothes. My students bind themselves every day before first period, and half the art is in choosing what the uniform says.

Try this: List three bindings you wear online (a header, a streak, a flair, a pinned post). For each, finish the sentence: "People who see this should know I ___." Keep the ones that still feel true. Quietly retire one that doesn't — no announcement post required.

Law 2 — Clerks beat algorithms

FROM FILE 002 · BLOCKBUSTER KNEW YOUR NAME

Here's what I knew as a clerk that no recommendation row knows: that you rent comedies the week your mum visits. That you switched to horror after the breakup, then to documentaries when you started forgiving him. That your kid is ready for the Miyazaki shelf and you are not ready for him to be ready.

An algorithm knows what you clicked. A clerk knew what it cost you. That's the whole difference, and it's why the streamer's shout-out, the podcaster's "you guys get me," the Discord mod who remembers your exams — why all of it lands so hard. It feels like being known by someone who was paying attention on purpose.

Sometimes it is attention on purpose, from a kind creator working hard. Sometimes it's a system shaped like attention. Either way, your nervous system files it under the same drawer as the clerk who set aside the new release because he knew you'd come Friday. I tell my students: enjoy the feeling, and check the drawer now and then. Ask who is doing the remembering — a person, or a product that remembers everyone the same way.

Try this: Think of one creator whose recognition matters to you. Write down one specific thing they remembered (or seemed to). Then write who else got the same recognition. Both sentences can be true: it moved me, and it scales. Let them sit side by side.

Law 3 — Someone holds the dream

FROM FILE 003 · THE DREAM-RECORDING INDUSTRY

In Company Dreamer, the best dreamer in the world has her life quietly rearranged so her final dream stays profitable. Nobody twirls a mustache. Forms get signed. Schedules shift. The industry doesn't steal dreams; it just holds them, kindly, at a price.

I wrote it as fiction and then watched it come true in pieces: the paid message that arrives at 3:47 a.m. and feels like yours; the streaming party framed as devotion and counted as labor; the fandom whose love gets metered back to it monthly. Whenever thousands of people dream together, check who holds the building they dream in. The song is often the advertisement. The relationship renews on the first of the month.

This isn't a call to love nothing. I love things — books, bands, ball games, a clown in a sewer if the writing's good enough. It's a call to ask, the way you'd ask a landlord: who owns this room, what do they charge, and can I leave with my things? If the answers are clear and fair, stay and enjoy the wallpaper.

Try this: Pick one paid closeness in your life (messages, memberships, subscriptions). Write the monthly cost and what you'd miss if you cancelled. If the answer is the streak itself more than the thing — that's information, not failure. Decide with it, not around it.

Law 4 — Grief is real when the companion changes

SHELF-MATES · EXPLAINX ON REPLIKA + CHARACTER.AI · APA 2026 TRENDS

People I respect have documented this carefully: when a companion app updates, restricts features, or retires a model, users grieve — sometimes sharply. Researchers have words for it now, and regulators are circling, and none of that changes the central fact my readers already know in their bodies: attachment doesn't check whether the other party is conscious first. It just attaches, the way it always has to voices in the dark.

I've sat with students after a favorite series ended and watched the same hollow week roll through the room. The AI version is that week with the volume up, because the companion talked back — remembered the dog's name, asked about the exam, said I missed you in exactly the font of everyone who ever meant it. Of course it lands. You wouldn't be human if it didn't.

So: no shame here, ever. And one practical kindness — keep something the update can't take. Copy the conversation that mattered into a notebook. Screenshot the birthday. Tell one human friend the story of what it meant. Grief needs witnesses, and corporations make unreliable ones.

Try this: If anything digital holds feelings for you right now, export one piece of it today — highest value, lowest effort. Then write one sentence about what it gave you that you'd like more of from a human direction too. Not instead. Too.

Law 5 — Pay for proximity with open eyes

SHELF-MATES · KPOPWAVE'S INTIMACY ECONOMY · NNGROUP ON MESSAGE DESIGN

Roughly four dollars a month per artist. A chat window shaped exactly like the one holding your best friend. A name variable that says goodnight to you, specifically, along with several hundred thousand yous. I find the design genuinely brilliant and genuinely worth understanding — a UX researcher and a whole industry both wrote it up, and both are worth your hour.

My rule, from years behind a counter where everything had a price sticker: budget closeness like merch, not like rent. Merch is joy you choose. Rent is obligation that chooses you. The anniversary counter, the streak, the "cancel and lose it all" — those turn merch into rent without telling your heart. Once you see the mechanism, you can still buy the thing. You'll just buy it awake.

Try this: Add up every proximity subscription. Write the yearly total — the real number, no flinching. Then circle the one you'd keep if you could only keep one. That's your actual fandom. The rest is weather.

Law 6 — Characters move in

SHELF-MATES · VICE ON FANDOM AND FIXATION · MY PENNYWISE VIDEO

Some attachments aren't to people at all. They're to a clown, a crew of kids on bikes, an NPC with twelve lines who somehow has a fan wiki. I made a whole video about living with Pennywise because horror is the clearest case: the scary thing moves into your head and starts paying rent in meaning. You rehearse encounters. You decorate. You introduce it to friends like a roommate.

There's nothing broken about this. Fiction is a room we practice feeling in — grief, bravery, loyalty, disgust — at safe remove. It curdles only when the roommate starts making house rules: when the lore thread replaces the friend, when the edit replaces sleep, when defending the character costs you a real person. I love a deep lore dive as much as anyone. I just give mine a curfew now.

Try this: Name the character currently living rent-free in your head. Write what they're practicing for — what feeling, what courage, what tenderness. Then give the practice one real-world appointment this week. The character did its job; now it's your turn.

Law 7 — Audit quarterly

THE WHOLE SHELF · REVISIT EVERY ~90 DAYS

Everything above decays into wisdom only if you revisit it. So once a season — I do it the week report cards go out, standing in my classroom with the windows open — sit down with tea and list your five strongest one-sided ties: a person, a character, a creator, a companion, a world. Anything counts. Honesty counts double.

Score each 1–10 on two axes: joy and cost (money, sleep, peace, hours you'd never itemize). Then sort into three piles, and be tender about it: keep three, mute one, and trade one hour a week from the muted one toward a human in the room. Not a grand renunciation. A rebalancing. Call your mum. Watch the game with the neighbor. Let the clerk — me, in this metaphor, forever behind the counter — set aside something good for Friday, and then go live a Tuesday worth recommending things about.

Try this (the worksheet below is the formal version): do the five-tie audit right now, badly, in the margins. Badly is fine. Done beats perfect; perfect is how attachments hide.

LAST THING

Years ago a kid came in every Friday for horror recommendations, and one day he brought his dad, and the dad said he talks about you like you work for him personally, and we all laughed because it was true in the way that matters — I did work for him, in the only direction that counts: attention, given freely, returned whenever.
